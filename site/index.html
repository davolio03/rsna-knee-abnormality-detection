Detecting 12 knee abnormalities from complete MRI studies

A study-level model for the RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection challenge. It reads every series of a knee MRI, weights slices separately for each abnormality, and reached a private ROC-AUC of 0.923. The largest late gain came from better training labels.

The problem

Each case is a full knee MRI study: several series (sagittal, coronal, axial; different sequences), each with dozens of slices. The task is to predict, for the whole study, the probability of 12 findings: ACL and MCL tears, medial and lateral meniscus tears, medial, lateral and patellofemoral osteoarthritis, effusion, synovitis, Baker's cyst, bone contusion and fracture. The metric is the mean ROC-AUC over the 12 targets.

Two things make it hard. A finding may be visible on only a few slices of one series, so the model must find the evidence without being told where it is. And the training data comes with radiology reports, not labels, so the targets themselves have to be built and checked.

Approach: attention over slices and series, per target

The model treats a study as a bag of slices grouped into series (multiple-instance learning). A shared ResNet-50, pretrained on radiology images (RadImageNet), encodes each slice. A linear head scores all 12 targets on every slice. Then, for each target separately, an attention layer decides which slices of a series matter, and a second one decides which series of the study matter. ACL evidence and effusion evidence live on different slices, so each target gets its own weights.

  • Input: every series, up to 16 uniformly spaced slices each, 224 × 224 px, intensities in [0, 1], with masks for padded pixels.
  • Objective: binary cross-entropy on soft labels (probabilities, not 0/1), masked where a label is unknown.
  • Training: Adam (head 1e-3, backbone 1e-5), 5 epochs, one study per step, BatchNorm frozen. The final epoch is fixed in advance; there is no early stopping on validation.
  • Final model: mean logits of 7 networks: three cross-validation fold models and four models trained on every labelled study with different seeds.

Cross-validation per target

Model changes were measured with 3-fold cross-validation on 4,298 studies, with folds grouped by normalized report so studies with identical reports never straddle a split. Fold-to-fold spread of the macro AUC is ±0.003, so a change needs a clear margin over that, ideally on every fold, to count.

Ligament and lateral meniscus tears are the hardest targets (0.81–0.83); synovitis, Baker's cyst and fracture are the easiest (0.90–0.91). These numbers are scored against noisy report-derived labels, so they are a proxy, not an estimate against radiologists.

Better labels beat a better model

The training data has radiology reports, not labels. Early models used labels that an LLM extracted from the report text alone. Those labels were noisy in a specific way: they marked far more positives than the images support (synovitis in 79% of observed cells against 16% in the image-aware labels) and left a quarter of all cells unknown.

The 7th-place team of the challenge published labels built from the images as well as the reports. Swapping them in, with the model, data and protocol unchanged, was the single largest gain since the attention model:

0.871 → 0.897
macro AUC against expert labels (58 studies the models never saw)
0.877 → 0.919

That +0.042 is more than 16 slices, layer3 fine-tuning and ensembling added together (+0.029).

How the decision was made

Cross-validation could not settle it. A model trained on new labels scores worse against the old labels and better against its own, whichever labels are right. So the comparison used the only expert-labelled data available, 58 studies with radiologist labels ("Gold-58"), kept out of all training and model selection until then. The decision rule was written before any result: adopt the new labels only if they win on Gold-58 and on the private leaderboard. Both held. Gold-58 predicted the direction and underestimated the size.

Engineering

  • Grouped folds. Study-level folds grouped by normalized report hash and balanced with an iterative multilabel allocator.
  • Pre-declared decisions. Every experiment fixes its epoch, metrics and decision rule before training. 59 experiment records and a decision log keep negative and inconclusive results.
  • Deterministic training. Kaggle T4 GPUs and a local RTX 3060 reproduce the same cross-validation AUC to four decimals. A run resumed from a checkpoint ends with weights identical to an uninterrupted one, which made it safe to split 12-hour trainings across time-limited sessions.
  • Two training machines. Folds and final models trained in parallel on a 6 GB laptop GPU and on Kaggle T4 × 2, with a runner that restores earlier outputs and resumes.
  • Pinned artifacts. Checkpoints, model exports and inference sources are verified by SHA-256 inside the offline Kaggle notebook, which scores the visible test studies in under a minute.
  • Tested code. 357 unit and regression tests on synthetic data run in CI; no patient data is ever committed.

Limitations

  • Training labels come from LLMs, not radiologists, and only 58 studies carry expert labels, so per-target expert AUCs are noisy.
  • All data comes from one challenge dataset; behaviour on other scanners, protocols or sites is unknown.
  • This is research code. The model is not a medical device and must not inform diagnosis or treatment.
  • Model weights are not published. They derive from challenge data under the RSNA MIRA license and from labels licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.
